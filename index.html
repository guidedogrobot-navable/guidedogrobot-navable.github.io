Abstract

Reliable recognition of accessibility-critical objects, such as accessible pedestrian signals, door-activation buttons, and handrails, is essential for technologies that support independent mobility for blind and low-vision (BLV) people. Yet these objects are often missing or underrepresented in existing vision datasets, and available data offer limited diversity, inconsistent annotations, and viewpoints that do not match assistive devices.

We introduce NavAble, a large-scale dataset and synthetic data generation pipeline for BLV navigation. NavAble combines 36,466 curated images with 5,681 collected image records, including 100 supplementary revolving-door frames, across 11 accessibility-critical categories. Its generator transforms web images into simulation-ready 3D assets and renders them in NVIDIA Isaac Sim. The current synthetic release contains 450,860 frames across 37 environments, with semantic segmentation, depth, surface normals, and 2D/3D bounding boxes.

Experiments across seven segmentation architectures reach 71.70% foreground mIoU on the paper's real-world benchmark. Adding synthetic data to real-only training improves five of the seven architectures by up to 7.19 mIoU points. Evaluation on a quadruped guide dog robot and a cane-shaped mobility-assistive device shows relative transfer gains on their egocentric viewpoints, while absolute accuracy remains insufficient for autonomous deployment.

Key Contributions

  • A dataset built around accessibility. Curated and newly collected real-world images with dense annotations for navigation-relevant infrastructure.
  • A scalable synthetic data generator. An image-to-3D asset pipeline and Isaac Sim extension for varying object appearance, environment, lighting, and camera trajectory.
  • Evaluation beyond a single viewpoint. Segmentation benchmarks and transfer evaluation on two different mobility-assistive robot platforms.

From Images to Synthetic Training Data

Specify an object, place an asset, and record a camera trajectory. Replay that trajectory across object variants, lighting conditions, and environments.

  1. Find and filter. Retrieve candidate object images and filter unsuitable examples with a vision-language model.
  2. Reconstruct assets. Localize and segment objects, then reconstruct textured 3D assets with SAM 3D Objects.
  3. Configure a scene. Set the asset placement and record a reusable camera trajectory.
  4. Render variations. Generate RGB, semantic segmentation, depth, surface normals, and 2D/3D bounding boxes.

Segmentation Evaluation

We benchmark seven segmentation architectures: SegMAN, Mask2Former, SAM 3, SAN, SegFormer, EncNet, and DeepLabv3+. Four training configurations separate the effects of collected data, curated data, and synthetic supervision, with 28 trained checkpoints released. The paper's main benchmark uses 1,482 real-world test images and reports foreground mIoU over 10 classes, excluding background and revolving door.

RealReal + CuratedReal + SyntheticReal + Curated + Synthetic

SegMAN with real, curated, and synthetic data reaches 71.70% foreground mIoU, compared with 26.07% for the strongest evaluated zero-shot baseline, CAT-Seg. Adding synthetic data to real-only training improves five of seven architectures, with a maximum gain of 7.19 mIoU points for SegMAN. EncNet also improves (+2.34 points); DeepLabv3+ and SAM 3 perform best on real-only training. Combining curated and synthetic data yields SegMAN's largest gain over real-only training: +9.50 points.

Results follow Tables 2 and 4 of the paper, using its training data and benchmark masks. Foreground mIoU excludes background; background-inclusive averages in the checkpoint documentation use a different denominator. Dataset counts above describe the current release.

Transfer to Assistive Robots

Offline evaluation on egocentric footage from Glide (157 images, 5 labelled classes) and a quadruped guide dog robot (155 images, 9 labelled classes).

Adding synthetic supervision to SegFormer improves foreground mIoU from 11.94% to 16.17% on Glide (+4.23 points) and from 21.56% to 23.08% on the quadruped (+1.52 points), as reported in Table 5 of the paper. Mean precision also improves on both platforms.

These are relative transfer gains. Absolute accuracy of 16.17%–23.08% foreground mIoU does not establish readiness for autonomous assistive navigation; broader class coverage, geographic diversity, and reliable closed-loop navigation remain open directions.
